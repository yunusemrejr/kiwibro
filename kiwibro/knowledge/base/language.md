# English grammar and usage

## Noun (part of speech)
A noun names a person, place, thing or idea, such as teacher, city, table or freedom. Nouns can be common or proper, countable or uncountable, and singular or plural.

## Verb (part of speech)
A verb says what someone or something does, or what state they are in, such as run, think or be. Every full sentence needs at least one verb, and the verb changes to show tense and person.

## Adjective (part of speech)
An adjective describes a noun, as in a red car or a long journey. In English adjectives do not change for number or gender, and they usually come before the noun or after a verb such as be or seem.

## Adverb (part of speech)
An adverb describes a verb, an adjective or another adverb, and often tells how, when or where something happens. Many end in -ly, such as quickly or rarely, but some do not, such as fast and well.

## Pronoun (part of speech)
A pronoun stands in place of a noun so the noun need not be repeated, such as I, it, she, them or who. Subject pronouns do the action, object pronouns receive it, and possessive pronouns show ownership.

## Preposition (part of speech)
A preposition shows the relation between a noun and the rest of the sentence, such as in, on, at, under or between. English prepositions are often idiomatic, so one says in the morning but at night.

## Conjunction (part of speech)
A conjunction joins words, phrases or clauses, such as and, but, or, because and although. Coordinating conjunctions join equal parts, and subordinating conjunctions begin a dependent clause.

## Interjection (part of speech)
An interjection is a short word or phrase that expresses feeling, such as oh, ouch, wow or well. It stands outside the grammar of the sentence and is usually followed by a comma or an exclamation mark.

## Present simple tense
The present simple describes habits, facts and regular events, as in "She works in a hospital" or "Water boils at 100 °C". It is formed with the base verb, adding -s for he, she and it.

## Present continuous tense
The present continuous describes something happening now or around now, as in "I am reading a book". It is formed with am, is or are plus the -ing form of the verb.

## Present perfect tense
The present perfect links the past to the present, as in "I have lost my keys", meaning I do not have them now. It is formed with have or has plus the past participle, and it often goes with just, already, yet, ever or never.

## Past simple tense
The past simple describes a finished action at a known past time, as in "We visited Rome in 2019". Regular verbs add -ed, and irregular verbs change form, such as go to went.

## Past continuous tense
The past continuous describes an action in progress at a past moment, as in "I was cooking when you called". It is formed with was or were plus the -ing form, and it often sets the scene for a shorter past action.

## Past perfect tense
The past perfect describes an action that happened before another past action, as in "She had left before I arrived". It is formed with had plus the past participle.

## Future simple tense (will)
The future simple describes a decision, prediction or promise, as in "I will call you tomorrow". It is formed with will plus the base verb; going to is often used for plans already made.

## Future continuous and future perfect
The future continuous describes something in progress at a future time, as in "At eight I will be travelling". The future perfect describes something finished before a future time, as in "By Friday I will have finished it".

## Articles a and an (indefinite)
A and an are used before a singular countable noun when it is not a particular one, as in a book or an idea. Use an before a vowel sound, so an hour but a university, because the choice follows the sound, not the letter.

## Article the (definite)
The is used when the listener knows which one is meant, as in the car outside or the sun. It is also used with unique things, superlatives and some names, such as the Pacific and the Netherlands.

## No article (zero article)
No article is used with plural and uncountable nouns in a general sense, as in "Cats like fish" or "Water is wet". It is also usual with most names of countries, languages, meals and sports.

## Countable and uncountable nouns
Countable nouns can be counted and take a plural, such as one apple, two apples. Uncountable nouns such as water, rice, information and advice have no plural and take a singular verb, so one says some information, not an information.

## Active and passive voice
In the active voice the subject does the action, as in "The dog bit the man". In the passive voice the action is done to the subject, as in "The man was bitten by the dog", which is formed with be plus the past participle.

## Zero conditional (if plus present)
The zero conditional states something always true, as in "If you heat water to 100 °C, it boils". Both parts use the present simple.

## First conditional (real future possibility)
The first conditional describes a likely future result, as in "If it rains, we will stay inside". The if part uses the present simple and the other part uses will.

## Second conditional (unreal present)
The second conditional describes an imagined present or unlikely future, as in "If I had more time, I would travel". The if part uses the past simple and the other part uses would plus the base verb.

## Third conditional (unreal past)
The third conditional describes something that did not happen and imagines its result, as in "If I had studied, I would have passed". It uses had plus the past participle with would have plus the past participle.

## Reported speech (saying what someone said)
Reported speech tells what someone said without quoting the exact words, so "I am tired" becomes "She said she was tired". Tenses usually move back one step and pronouns and time words change.

## Comma (punctuation)
A comma marks a short pause and separates items in a list, clauses in a sentence and words added to the beginning or end. It also separates a direct quotation from the rest of the sentence, as in "He said, 'Wait'".

## Full stop (period)
A full stop ends a statement, and it is also used in abbreviations such as etc. In American English it is called a period, and in web addresses it is called a dot.

## Question mark
A question mark ends a direct question, as in "Where are you going?". It is not used for an indirect question, as in "I wonder where he went".

## Exclamation mark
An exclamation mark ends a sentence that shows strong feeling, such as surprise, anger or excitement. It is used sparingly in formal writing.

## Semicolon (punctuation)
A semicolon joins two complete sentences that are closely related, as in "It was raining; we stayed in". It can also separate items in a list when the items themselves contain commas.

## Colon (punctuation)
A colon introduces a list, an explanation or a quotation, as in "You need three things: paper, pen and glue". It should follow a complete sentence, not a verb or preposition.

## Apostrophe (punctuation)
An apostrophe shows that letters have been left out, as in don't for do not, or shows possession, as in the girl's coat. For a plural ending in -s, the apostrophe goes after the s, as in the girls' coats.

## Hyphen (punctuation)
A hyphen joins words into one, as in well-known or mother-in-law, and it can break a word at the end of a line. Hyphens are shorter than dashes and have no spaces around them.

## En dash (punctuation)
An en dash is slightly longer than a hyphen and is used for ranges, as in pages 10-20 or 1990-1995, and between words of equal weight, as in the London-Paris train. It is about as wide as the letter n.

## Em dash (punctuation)
An em dash is the longest dash and marks a break in a sentence, adding an aside or a strong pause, as in "He arrived late — again". It can often replace a colon or a pair of brackets.

## Quotation marks (punctuation)
Quotation marks enclose the exact words someone said or a title of a short work. British English often uses single marks first, as in 'hello', and American English double marks, as in "hello", with the other kind inside.

## Capitalisation rules
Capital letters begin every sentence, every proper name such as Anna or London, the days and months, and the pronoun I. Common nouns, the seasons and job titles in ordinary use are not capitalised.

## Their, there and they're
Their shows possession, as in their house; there means a place or introduces a sentence, as in over there or there is; they're is short for they are. A quick test is to replace the word with they are to see if it still makes sense.

## Your and you're
Your shows possession, as in your coat, and you're is short for you are. If you can write you are in the sentence, use you're.

## Its and it's
Its shows possession, as in the dog wagged its tail, and it's is short for it is or it has. Unlike other possessives, its takes no apostrophe.

## Affect and effect
Affect is usually a verb meaning to influence, as in the rain affected the game. Effect is usually a noun meaning a result, as in the effect of the rain; as a verb it means to bring about, as in to effect a change.

## Then and than
Then relates to time, as in we ate and then left. Than compares, as in bigger than before.

## Who and whom
Who is used for the subject of a verb, as in who called?, and whom for the object, as in whom did you call?. In everyday speech who is often used for both, but whom follows a preposition, as in to whom.

## Fewer and less
Fewer is used with things you can count, as in fewer cars, and less with uncountable amounts, as in less traffic. Less is also used with measurements and money, as in less than five kilometres.

## Lie and lay
Lie means to rest or recline, as in lie down, and its past is lay. Lay means to put something down and needs an object, as in lay the book on the table, and its past is laid.

## Lose and loose
Lose is a verb meaning to no longer have something, as in lose a key, with past lost. Loose is an adjective meaning not tight, as in a loose screw, and rhymes with goose.

## Principal and principle
Principal means main or the head of a school, as in the principal reason. Principle means a rule or belief, as in a matter of principle.

## Complement and compliment
Complement means something that completes or goes well with another, as in the wine complements the meal. Compliment means praise, as in she paid me a compliment.

## i.e. and e.g. (that is and for example)
i.e. means that is, and it restates something more precisely. e.g. means for example, and it gives one or more examples; neither should be confused with the other.

## Who's and whose
Who's is short for who is or who has, as in who's coming?. Whose shows possession, as in whose bag is this?.

## To, too and two
To is a preposition or part of the infinitive, as in go to school or to run. Too means also or excessively, as in too late, and two is the number 2.

## Accept and except
Accept is a verb meaning to receive or agree to, as in accept an offer. Except is a preposition or conjunction meaning apart from, as in everyone except me.

## Advice and advise
Advice is a noun meaning a suggestion, as in good advice. Advise is a verb meaning to give advice, as in I advise you to wait.

## Practice and practise
In British English practice is the noun, as in a doctor's practice, and practise is the verb, as in practise the piano. In American English practice is used for both.

## British and American spelling (-our and -or)
British spelling keeps -our in words such as colour, favour and behaviour, while American spelling drops the u to give color, favor and behavior. Words such as glamour and saviour keep the u in both.

## British and American spelling (-ise and -ize)
British English uses both -ise and -ize, with -ise more common, as in organise or organize; American English uses -ize, as in organize. Some words are always -ise in both, such as advertise and exercise.

## British and American spelling (-re and -er)
British English ends some words in -re, such as centre, metre and theatre, and American English uses -er, as in center, meter and theater. Note that meter in American English is both the unit and the measuring device.

## British and American spelling (double letters)
British English doubles the l before a suffix in words such as travelled, cancelled and modelling, while American English uses traveled, canceled and modeling. American English also writes defense and offense where British English has defence and offence.

## Irregular plurals (man, child, mouse)
Some nouns form the plural by changing their vowel or adding -en, such as man to men, woman to women, child to children and mouse to mice. Others do not change at all, such as sheep, deer and fish.

## Irregular plurals from Latin and Greek (crisis, datum)
Many words taken from Latin and Greek keep their original plurals, such as crisis to crises, datum to data, criterion to criteria and phenomenon to phenomena. Some are now used with English plurals too, such as formulas alongside formulae.

## Etc. (and so on)
Etc. is short for et cetera, Latin for "and the rest", and ends a list without giving every item. It already means "and", so it should not be written as "and etc.".

## Et al. (and others)
Et al. is short for et alii, Latin for "and others", and is used in references to a work with several authors. It follows the first author's name, as in Smith et al.

## Vs. and versus (against)
Vs. is a short form of versus, meaning against, and is used in sport, law and comparisons. It is often written without the full stop as vs, and the full word versus is preferred in formal writing.

## a.m. and p.m. (before and after noon)
a.m. comes from Latin ante meridiem, before midday, and p.m. from post meridiem, after midday. The 12-hour clock runs from 12 a.m., midnight, to 11:59 p.m., just before midnight.

## AD and BC (year numbering)
AD stands for anno Domini, in the year of the Lord, and counts years after the traditional birth of Christ; BC counts years before it and is written after the number, as in 44 BC. There is no year zero.

## CE and BCE (common era)
CE means Common Era and BCE means Before Common Era, and they match AD and BC without a religious reference. Both are written after the number, as in 476 CE and 44 BCE.

## Simile (figure of speech)
A simile compares two things using like or as, as in "as brave as a lion" or "runs like the wind". It makes a description vivid by pointing to something familiar.

## Metaphor (figure of speech)
A metaphor says one thing is another to suggest a likeness, as in "the world is a stage" or "a sea of troubles". Unlike a simile it does not use like or as.

## Irony (figure of speech)
Irony says something different from what is meant or expected, often for effect, as when a fire station burns down. Verbal irony says the opposite of what is meant, and dramatic irony is when the audience knows more than the characters.

## Hyperbole (figure of speech)
Hyperbole is deliberate exaggeration for emphasis, as in "I have told you a million times" or "this bag weighs a ton". It is not meant to be taken literally.

## Alliteration (figure of speech)
Alliteration is the repetition of the same sound at the start of nearby words, as in "sweet silver song" or "big bad wolf". It is common in poetry, slogans and brand names.

## Idiom (figure of speech)
An idiom is a phrase whose meaning cannot be worked out from its individual words, such as "break the ice" or "under the weather". Idioms are fixed expressions and often cannot be translated word for word.

## Parts of a letter or email (greeting and sign-off)
A formal letter begins with a greeting such as Dear Ms Smith, followed by the body and a closing such as Yours sincerely when the name is known, or Yours faithfully when it is not. An email has a subject line, a greeting, the message, a sign-off and a signature.

## Writing dates (British and American order)
British and most European writing puts the day first, as in 3 May 2024 or 03/05/2024. American writing puts the month first, as in May 3, 2024 or 05/03/2024, which is why writing the month as a name avoids confusion.

## Writing numbers (figures or words)
Numbers from one to nine are usually written in words and larger numbers in figures, as in three apples and 245 people. Figures are used for measurements, money, times, dates and statistics, and a sentence should not begin with a figure.

## Writing large numbers (thousands separator)
In English a comma or a space separates thousands, as in 1,000,000 or 1 000 000, and a full stop marks the decimal, as in 3.5. Some countries use a comma for the decimal, so 3,5 means the same as 3.5.
