# Physics and measurement reference

## SI base units (the seven base units)
The International System of Units (SI) is built on seven base units: the metre for length, the kilogram for mass, the second for time, the ampere for electric current, the kelvin for temperature, the mole for amount of substance and the candela for luminous intensity. Every other SI unit is defined from these seven.

## Metre (unit of length)
The metre is the SI base unit of length, symbol m. It is the unit in which distances and sizes are measured in science, from the width of a hair to the distance to a star. Lengths in other units, such as centimetres or kilometres, are defined as multiples or fractions of the metre.

## Kilogram (unit of mass)
The kilogram is the SI base unit of mass, symbol kg. Mass measures how much matter an object contains, not how heavy it feels. A litre of water has a mass of about one kilogram, which gives a rough sense of the unit.

## Second (unit of time)
The second is the SI base unit of time, symbol s. It is defined by counting vibrations of the caesium atom, which gives a stable standard the world's clocks agree on. Minutes, hours and days are multiples of the second.

## Ampere (unit of electric current)
The ampere is the SI base unit of electric current, symbol A. Current is the flow of electric charge past a point, measured in coulombs per second. A common household appliance draws a few amperes; a small electronic device draws a fraction of one.

## Kelvin (unit of temperature)
The kelvin is the SI base unit of temperature, symbol K. It starts at absolute zero, the coldest possible temperature, so it has no negative values. A change of one kelvin equals a change of one degree Celsius; the two scales differ only in where they start.

## Mole (SI base unit for amount of substance)
The mole is the SI base unit for the amount of a substance, symbol mol. One mole contains 6.02214076 * 10^23 particles, a number called the Avogadro constant. Chemists use it because counting individual atoms or molecules is impossible in practice.

## Candela (unit of luminous intensity)
The candela is the SI base unit of luminous intensity, symbol cd. It describes how bright a light source is in a given direction. It is the one base unit based on human vision rather than on a purely physical quantity.

## Newton (unit of force)
The newton is the SI unit of force, symbol N. One newton is the force that gives a mass of one kilogram an acceleration of one metre per second squared. An apple resting in your hand presses on it with about one newton of force.

## Joule (unit of energy and work)
The joule is the SI unit of energy and work, symbol J. One joule is the work done when a force of one newton moves an object one metre. Energy is also measured in calories, with one calorie equal to about 4.18 joules.

## Watt (unit of power)
The watt is the SI unit of power, symbol W. One watt is one joule of energy used per second. A typical LED bulb uses several watts, a kettle over a thousand watts, and a large power station millions of watts.

## Pascal (unit of pressure)
The pascal is the SI unit of pressure, symbol Pa. One pascal is a force of one newton pressing on one square metre. Atmospheric pressure at sea level is about 101,325 pascals, often written as about 101 kPa.

## Hertz (unit of frequency)
The hertz is the SI unit of frequency, symbol Hz. One hertz means one cycle or vibration per second. A sound at 440 Hz vibrates 440 times a second.

## Volt (unit of electrical potential difference)
The volt is the SI unit of electrical potential difference, symbol V. One volt is one joule of energy per coulomb of charge. It measures the push that drives current through a circuit.

## Ohm (unit of electrical resistance)
The ohm is the SI unit of electrical resistance, symbol written as the Greek letter omega. One ohm is the resistance that allows one ampere of current when one volt is applied. Higher resistance means less current for the same voltage.

## Coulomb (unit of electric charge)
The coulomb is the SI unit of electric charge, symbol C. One coulomb is the charge carried past a point by a current of one ampere in one second. The charge on a single electron is tiny, about 1.6 * 10^-19 coulombs.

## Farad (unit of capacitance)
The farad is the SI unit of capacitance, symbol F. One farad holds one coulomb of charge when one volt is applied. It is a very large unit, so most capacitors are measured in microfarads, nanofarads or picofarads.

## Tesla (unit of magnetic flux density)
The tesla is the SI unit of magnetic flux density, symbol T. It describes the strength of a magnetic field. A fridge magnet produces a field of a few thousandths of a tesla, while an MRI scanner uses fields of a few tesla.

## SI prefixes from pico to tera
SI prefixes scale units by powers of ten: pico is 10^-12, nano 10^-9, micro 10^-6, milli 10^-3, centi 10^-2, deci 10^-1, deca 10^1, hecto 10^2, kilo 10^3, mega 10^6, giga 10^9 and tera 10^12. A kilometre is 1000 metres and a nanometre is a billionth of a metre.

## Speed of light in vacuum
The speed of light in a vacuum is exactly 299,792,458 metres per second, about 300,000 kilometres per second. It is the fastest speed at which information or matter can travel. Light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to reach Earth.

## Standard gravity
Standard gravity, the acceleration due to gravity at the Earth's surface used as a standard value, is exactly 9.80665 metres per second squared. In everyday calculations it is often rounded to 9.8 or 9.81. A falling object speeds up by about 9.8 metres per second every second, ignoring air resistance.

## Planck constant
The Planck constant is the fundamental constant of quantum physics, about 6.626 * 10^-34 joule seconds. It links the energy of a photon to its frequency: energy = Planck constant * frequency. Its small size is why quantum effects are not noticed in everyday life.

## Elementary charge
The elementary charge is the size of the charge on a single proton, about 1.602 * 10^-19 coulombs. An electron carries the same size of charge with the opposite sign. All observed free charges are whole multiples of this value.

## Avogadro constant
The Avogadro constant is the number of particles in one mole of a substance, exactly 6.02214076 * 10^23 per mole. It lets chemists count atoms by weighing amounts of material. It is named after the scientist Amedeo Avogadro.

## Boltzmann constant
The Boltzmann constant links temperature to energy at the level of single particles, about 1.381 * 10^-23 joules per kelvin. It appears in the ideal gas law and in the formula for the average kinetic energy of particles in a gas.

## Absolute zero
Absolute zero is the lowest possible temperature, 0 kelvin, equal to -273.15 degrees Celsius. At this temperature particles have the least possible thermal energy. It cannot be reached exactly, though scientists have come extremely close in laboratories.

## Newton's first law (law of inertia)
Newton's first law says an object at rest stays at rest and an object moving at a constant speed in a straight line keeps doing so unless a net force acts on it. This tendency to resist changes in motion is called inertia. It is why you lurch forward when a bus stops suddenly.

## Newton's second law (F = m * a)
Newton's second law says the net force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration: F = m * a. A larger force gives a larger acceleration, and a heavier object accelerates less for the same force. The acceleration is in the direction of the net force.

## Newton's third law (action and reaction)
Newton's third law says that for every force there is an equal and opposite force. When you push on a wall, the wall pushes back on you with the same size of force. The two forces act on different objects, which is why they do not cancel out.

## Gravity
Gravity is the force of attraction between any two objects with mass. It gets stronger as the masses grow and weaker as the distance between them grows. Gravity keeps planets in orbit around the Sun and holds you on the ground.

## Mass and weight
Mass is the amount of matter in an object and stays the same wherever the object is. Weight is the force of gravity on that mass, so it changes with location: the same person weighs less on the Moon than on Earth. Weight = mass * gravitational acceleration.

## Speed and velocity
Speed is how fast an object moves, calculated as distance divided by time. Velocity is speed in a stated direction, so it is a vector. A car going round a bend at a steady 50 km/h has constant speed but changing velocity.

## Acceleration
Acceleration is the rate at which velocity changes, calculated as change in velocity divided by time. It is measured in metres per second squared. Speeding up, slowing down and changing direction all count as acceleration.

## Equations of motion (constant acceleration)
For motion with constant acceleration a, starting speed u, final speed v, displacement s and time t: v = u + a*t, s = u*t + (1/2)*a*t^2 and v^2 = u^2 + 2*a*s. These give distance and speed at any time without drawing a graph.

## Force
Force is a push or a pull that can change an object's speed, direction or shape. It is measured in newtons. Forces are vectors, so both size and direction matter, and several forces on an object add together to give a net force.

## Friction
Friction is the force that resists sliding between two surfaces in contact. It acts opposite to the direction of motion and turns kinetic energy into heat. Friction lets us walk and brakes a car, but it also wastes energy in machines.

## Work (physics)
In physics, work is done when a force moves an object in the direction of the force. Work = force * distance moved along the force, measured in joules. Holding a heavy box still does no work, because nothing moves.

## Kinetic energy
Kinetic energy is the energy an object has because it is moving. It is calculated as kinetic energy = (1/2) * mass * speed^2. Doubling the speed gives four times the kinetic energy, which is why braking distance grows quickly with speed.

## Gravitational potential energy
Gravitational potential energy is energy stored in an object because of its height above a reference level. Near the Earth's surface it is calculated as mass * gravitational acceleration * height. Lifting an object stores energy that is released when it falls.

## Conservation of energy
The law of conservation of energy says energy cannot be created or destroyed, only changed from one form to another. The total energy of a closed system stays the same. Some energy often ends up as heat spread into the surroundings, which is why machines are never perfectly efficient.

## Power (physics)
Power is the rate at which energy is transferred or work is done. Power = work / time, and it is measured in watts. A more powerful engine does the same work in less time.

## Momentum
Momentum is mass times velocity, measured in kilogram metres per second. It is a vector, so direction matters. In a closed system the total momentum before a collision equals the total momentum after it.

## Pressure
Pressure is force spread over an area: pressure = force / area, measured in pascals. The same force over a smaller area gives higher pressure, which is why a sharp knife cuts easily and snowshoes stop you sinking.

## Density
Density is mass per unit volume: density = mass / volume, measured in kilograms per cubic metre. Water has a density of about 1000 kilograms per cubic metre. An object less dense than a fluid floats in it, and a denser one sinks.

## Ohm's law
Ohm's law says voltage = current * resistance, written V = I * R. It holds for many conductors at a steady temperature. If resistance stays the same, doubling the voltage doubles the current.

## Electrical power
Electrical power is voltage times current: power = V * I, measured in watts. It can also be written as power = I^2 * R or power = V^2 / R using Ohm's law. A device rated at 230 volts and 2 amperes uses 460 watts.

## Resistors in series
When resistors are connected in series, the same current flows through each and the total resistance is the sum: R = R1 + R2 + ... . Adding a resistor in series increases the total resistance and reduces the current.

## Resistors in parallel
When resistors are connected in parallel, the voltage across each is the same and the currents add. The total resistance is found from 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ... and is always smaller than the smallest single resistor. Parallel connections are used in homes so each device works on its own.

## Temperature scales (Celsius, Fahrenheit, Kelvin)
Celsius sets water freezing at 0 degrees and boiling at 100 degrees at sea level. Fahrenheit sets freezing at 32 degrees and boiling at 212 degrees. Kelvin starts at absolute zero and uses the same step size as Celsius.

## Temperature conversion formulas
Celsius to Fahrenheit: F = C * 9/5 + 32. Fahrenheit to Celsius: C = (F - 32) * 5/9. Celsius to Kelvin: K = C + 273.15. Kelvin to Celsius: C = K - 273.15. So 100 degrees Celsius is 212 degrees Fahrenheit and 373.15 kelvin.

## States of matter
The three common states of matter are solid, liquid and gas. A solid has a fixed shape and volume, a liquid has a fixed volume but takes the shape of its container, and a gas fills its container. Heating a substance can change its state.

## Conduction (heat transfer)
Conduction is heat transfer through direct contact, as energy passes from particle to particle. Metals conduct heat well because they have free electrons; wood and plastic conduct poorly. A metal spoon in hot soup soon feels hot.

## Convection (heat transfer)
Convection is heat transfer by the movement of a fluid. Warm liquid or gas becomes less dense and rises, while cooler fluid sinks, setting up a circulating current. It is how a room is warmed by a radiator and how boiling water mixes.

## Radiation (heat transfer)
Radiation is heat transfer by electromagnetic waves, mainly infrared, and needs no medium at all. It is how the Sun's heat reaches Earth through space. Dark, dull surfaces absorb and emit radiation well; shiny surfaces reflect it.

## Waves
A wave is a disturbance that carries energy from place to place without carrying matter along with it. In a transverse wave the vibration is at right angles to the direction of travel; in a longitudinal wave it is along the direction of travel. Sound is longitudinal and light is transverse.

## Frequency
Frequency is the number of wave cycles that pass a point each second, measured in hertz. A higher frequency means more vibrations per second. For sound, frequency sets the pitch; for light, it sets the colour.

## Wavelength
Wavelength is the distance between two matching points on a wave, such as two crests, measured in metres. It is usually shown by the Greek letter lambda. Longer wavelength means lower frequency when the wave speed is fixed.

## Wave equation
The wave equation links speed, frequency and wavelength: speed = frequency * wavelength. A sound wave of 440 Hz travelling at 340 metres per second has a wavelength of about 0.77 metres. The equation works for all waves, including light.

## Amplitude
Amplitude is the maximum displacement of a wave from its resting position. For sound it sets how loud the sound is; for light it sets how bright it is. It does not change the frequency or wavelength.

## Electromagnetic spectrum
The electromagnetic spectrum, from longest wavelength and lowest energy to shortest, is: radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays. Visible light is a tiny band in the middle, running from red through orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo to violet.

## Sound versus light
Sound is a mechanical wave that needs a medium such as air, water or metal, so it cannot travel through a vacuum. Light is an electromagnetic wave that travels through a vacuum at about 300,000 kilometres per second. Sound travels much more slowly, about 343 metres per second in air.

## Reflection
Reflection is a wave bouncing off a surface. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, both measured from the normal, the line at right angles to the surface. Mirrors reflect light this way, and echoes are reflected sound.

## Refraction
Refraction is the bending of a wave when it passes from one medium into another where its speed differs. It bends towards the normal when slowing down and away from the normal when speeding up. A straw looks bent in a glass of water because of refraction.

## E = mc^2 (mass-energy equivalence)
E = mc^2 is Einstein's equation saying that mass and energy are two forms of the same thing. E is energy, m is mass and c is the speed of light. Because c is very large, a tiny amount of mass holds an enormous amount of energy, which is how nuclear reactions release power.

## Half-life
The half-life of a radioactive substance is the time it takes for half of its radioactive atoms to decay. After one half-life half remain, after two a quarter, and so on. Half-lives range from fractions of a second to billions of years depending on the substance.

## Buoyancy and Archimedes' principle
Archimedes' principle says an object in a fluid feels an upward force equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces. If that force equals the object's weight the object floats; if it is less, the object sinks. It explains why heavy steel ships float.

## Scalars and vectors
A scalar is a quantity with size only, such as mass, time or temperature. A vector is a quantity with size and direction, such as force, velocity or displacement. Vectors add by direction as well as size, so two forces can partly cancel.

## Distance and displacement
Distance is how far an object travels in total, whatever the direction. Displacement is the straight-line change in position from start to finish, with a direction. Walk all the way round a track and the distance is large, but the displacement is zero.

## Average speed
Average speed is the total distance travelled divided by the total time taken. A car covering 150 kilometres in 2 hours has an average speed of 75 kilometres per hour. It says nothing about the speed at any particular moment.

## Electric charge
Electric charge is a property of matter that comes in two kinds, positive and negative. Like charges repel and opposite charges attract. Charge is measured in coulombs, and the smallest free amount is the charge of one electron.

## Electric current
Electric current is the rate at which electric charge flows past a point: current = charge / time, measured in amperes. Current flows in a circuit when a voltage drives it. Conventional current direction is from positive to negative.

## Voltage (potential difference)
Voltage, or potential difference, is the energy transferred per unit of charge between two points in a circuit: voltage = energy / charge, measured in volts. It is the push that drives current. A battery's voltage stays roughly steady as it supplies current.

## Resistance
Resistance is how much a component opposes the flow of current: resistance = voltage / current, measured in ohms. Thinner, longer wires have more resistance. Resistance turns electrical energy into heat, which is useful in a heater and wasteful in a cable.

## Specific heat capacity
Specific heat capacity is the energy needed to raise the temperature of one kilogram of a material by one kelvin, measured in joules per kilogram per kelvin. Water has a high specific heat capacity, which is why it heats and cools slowly.

## Latent heat
Latent heat is the energy needed to change a substance's state without changing its temperature, such as melting ice or boiling water. During the change the energy goes into loosening the particles, not into raising the temperature.

## Nuclear fission and fusion
Nuclear fission splits a heavy nucleus, such as uranium, into lighter ones and releases energy. Nuclear fusion joins light nuclei, such as hydrogen, into heavier ones and releases more energy per unit mass. The Sun is powered by fusion, and current nuclear power stations use fission.

## Doppler effect
The Doppler effect is the change in observed wave frequency when the source and observer move relative to each other. The pitch of a siren rises as it approaches and falls as it passes. Astronomers use the same effect with light to tell whether a star is moving towards or away from us.

## Hooke's law
Hooke's law says the stretch of a spring is directly proportional to the force pulling it, up to a limit: force = spring constant * extension. Stretch a spring twice as far and it pulls back twice as hard. Beyond the elastic limit a spring stays permanently deformed.
