# Astronomy reference

## Solar System
The Solar System is the Sun together with everything bound to it by gravity: eight planets, dwarf planets, moons, asteroids, comets, dust and gas. It formed about 4.6 billion years ago. The Sun holds almost all of its mass.

## Mercury
Mercury is the first planet from the Sun and the smallest planet in the Solar System. It has almost no atmosphere and a heavily cratered, rocky surface, and it has no moons. One orbit takes about 88 Earth days.

## Venus
Venus is the second planet from the Sun and similar in size to Earth. It has a thick atmosphere of carbon dioxide that traps heat, making it the hottest planet, with a surface hot enough to melt lead. It spins in the opposite direction to most planets.

## Earth
Earth is the third planet from the Sun and the largest of the rocky planets. It is the only planet known to support life, and about 71% of its surface is covered by water. It has one large natural satellite, the Moon.

## Mars
Mars is the fourth planet from the Sun and is often called the red planet because of iron oxide dust on its surface. It has two small moons, Phobos and Deimos, and the largest volcano in the Solar System, Olympus Mons. A day on Mars lasts about 24.6 hours and a year about 687 Earth days.

## Jupiter
Jupiter is the fifth planet from the Sun and the largest planet in the Solar System. It is a gas giant made mostly of hydrogen and helium, and it has a huge storm called the Great Red Spot. It has many moons.

## Saturn
Saturn is the sixth planet from the Sun and the second largest. It is a gas giant famous for its bright rings, which are made of ice and rock. It is less dense than water, so it would float if there were a big enough ocean.

## Uranus
Uranus is the seventh planet from the Sun and an ice giant. It is tilted so far over that it appears to roll around the Sun on its side. It was discovered with a telescope in 1781.

## Neptune
Neptune is the eighth planet from the Sun and the farthest of the planets. It is an ice giant with a deep blue colour and very fast winds. It was discovered in 1846 after astronomers predicted where it must be.

## Order of the planets from the Sun
The eight planets in order from the Sun are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. A memory phrase for the order is "My Very Educated Mother Just Served Us Noodles". The four inner planets are rocky and the four outer ones are giants.

## Largest planet (Jupiter)
Jupiter is the largest planet in the Solar System, more massive than all the other planets put together. It is a gas giant with a diameter about 11 times Earth's. It is also the fastest-spinning planet, with a day of about 10 hours.

## Smallest planet (Mercury)
Mercury is the smallest planet in the Solar System, slightly smaller than the largest moons of Jupiter and Saturn. It is also the closest planet to the Sun. Being small and having almost no atmosphere, it has extreme temperature swings between day and night.

## Hottest planet (Venus)
Venus is the hottest planet in the Solar System, with a surface temperature high enough to melt lead. It is hotter than Mercury even though it is farther from the Sun. The cause is its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere, which traps heat.

## The Sun
The Sun is the star at the centre of the Solar System and the source of most of its energy. It is a ball of hot gas, mostly hydrogen and helium, and it is about 4.6 billion years old. Light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to reach Earth.

## The Moon
The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite, orbiting it at an average distance of about 384,400 kilometres. It always shows the same face to Earth because its rotation and orbit take the same time. Its gravity causes the ocean tides.

## Dwarf planet
A dwarf planet is a round body that orbits the Sun but has not cleared its neighbourhood of other objects, so it does not count as a full planet. Pluto, Ceres and Eris are dwarf planets. The term was defined by astronomers in 2006.

## Pluto
Pluto is a dwarf planet in the Kuiper belt beyond Neptune, discovered in 1930. It was counted as the ninth planet until 2006, when it was reclassified as a dwarf planet. It has five known moons, the largest called Charon.

## Ceres
Ceres is the largest object in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter and is classed as a dwarf planet. It was the first asteroid discovered, in 1801. It is roughly spherical and may contain water ice.

## Eris
Eris is a dwarf planet far beyond Neptune, similar in size to Pluto. Its discovery in 2005 led astronomers to tighten the definition of a planet in 2006. It is one of the most massive known dwarf planets.

## Asteroid belt
The asteroid belt is a region between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter where many rocky objects orbit the Sun. Its objects are called asteroids, and it also contains the dwarf planet Ceres. The total mass of the belt is far less than that of the Moon.

## Kuiper belt
The Kuiper belt is a ring of icy bodies beyond Neptune, including Pluto and many other small objects. It is much wider and more massive than the asteroid belt. Comets with short orbits come from this region.

## Comet
A comet is a small icy body that orbits the Sun and develops a glowing tail when it comes close. The heat turns ice into gas, and the tail always points away from the Sun. Halley's comet returns about every 76 years.

## Meteor, meteoroid and meteorite
A meteoroid is a small piece of rock or dust in space, and a meteor is the streak of light it makes when it burns up in the atmosphere. A meteorite is the part that survives and lands on the ground. Most meteors burn up completely and never reach the surface.

## Star
A star is a huge ball of gas held together by gravity, shining because of nuclear fusion in its core. The Sun is the nearest star to Earth. Stars differ in size, temperature and colour, from cool red stars to hot blue ones.

## White dwarf
A white dwarf is the small, dense, cooling core left behind when a star like the Sun runs out of fuel. It is about the size of Earth but holds a large part of the star's mass. Over a very long time it fades and cools.

## Supernova
A supernova is the enormous explosion that ends the life of a massive star, or of a white dwarf that gains too much matter. For a short time it can outshine a whole galaxy. Heavy elements made in stars are scattered into space by supernovae.

## Nebula
A nebula is a large cloud of gas and dust in space. Some nebulae are where new stars are born, and others are the remains of dying stars. They often glow because nearby stars light them up.

## Black hole
A black hole is a region where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape once it gets too close. The boundary of no return is called the event horizon. Black holes form when very massive stars collapse at the end of their lives.

## Pulsar
A pulsar is a rapidly spinning neutron star that sends out beams of radiation, which appear as regular pulses as the star turns. The pulses can be as quick as milliseconds apart. Pulsars were discovered in 1967.

## Galaxy
A galaxy is a huge system of stars, gas, dust and dark matter held together by gravity. Our own galaxy is the Milky Way. Galaxies come in shapes such as spiral, elliptical and irregular.

## Milky Way
The Milky Way is the galaxy that contains the Solar System. It is a spiral galaxy about 100,000 light-years across, and the Sun lies in one of its outer arms. From a dark place it appears as a faint band of light across the night sky.

## Light-year
A light-year is the distance light travels in one year in a vacuum, about 9.46 trillion kilometres. It is a unit of distance, not of time. The nearest star after the Sun is about 4.2 light-years away.

## Astronomical unit
An astronomical unit is the average distance from the Earth to the Sun, about 150 million kilometres. It is used to measure distances within the Solar System. Neptune orbits about 30 astronomical units from the Sun.

## Orbit
An orbit is the curved path an object follows around another because of gravity. Planets orbit the Sun in almost circular ellipses, and moons orbit planets. An orbit stays steady when the object's speed balances the pull of gravity.

## Rotation and revolution
Rotation is an object spinning on its own axis, which for Earth causes day and night. Revolution is the motion of one object around another, which for Earth around the Sun causes the year. A planet's day is one rotation and its year is one revolution.

## Why there are seasons
Seasons happen because the Earth's axis is tilted by about 23.5 degrees as it orbits the Sun. Different parts of the planet receive sunlight at steeper angles and for longer each day at different times of year. Seasons are not caused by the distance to the Sun.

## Solstice and equinox
A solstice is when the Sun reaches its highest or lowest point in the sky, giving the longest and shortest days of the year, around 21 June and 21 December. An equinox is when day and night are about equal everywhere, around 21 March and 22 September.

## Phases of the Moon
The Moon shows phases because we see different amounts of its sunlit half as it orbits Earth. The cycle runs new, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full, waning gibbous, last quarter and waning crescent. A full cycle takes about 29.5 days.

## Solar eclipse
A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth and blocks some or all of the Sun's light. It can be total, partial or annular depending on the alignment. Never look directly at the Sun during an eclipse without proper filters.

## Lunar eclipse
A lunar eclipse happens when Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon, so Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. It can be seen from the whole night side of Earth at once. The Moon often turns a dull red or copper colour during the eclipse.

## Tides
Tides are the regular rise and fall of sea level caused mainly by the Moon's gravity, with help from the Sun. Most coasts have two high tides and two low tides a day. Spring tides at new and full Moon are larger than neap tides.

## Constellation
A constellation is a pattern of stars as seen from Earth, given a name and boundaries by astronomers. There are 88 official constellations covering the whole sky. The stars in one are usually at very different distances from us.

## Big Bang
The Big Bang is the scientific model in which the universe began in an extremely hot, dense state and has been expanding ever since, about 13.8 billion years ago. It was not an explosion into empty space: space itself has been stretching. The model explains why distant galaxies are moving away from us.

## Cosmic microwave background
The cosmic microwave background is faint radiation filling the whole sky, left over from the early universe. It was released when the young universe cooled enough to become transparent. It was discovered in the 1960s and is a key piece of evidence for the Big Bang.

## Natural satellite
A natural satellite is an object that orbits a planet or dwarf planet, commonly called a moon. Earth has one, Mars has two, and the giant planets have many. Most natural satellites are made of rock or ice.

## Aurora
An aurora is a glow in the night sky caused when charged particles from the Sun hit gases in Earth's atmosphere near the poles. The aurora borealis is in the north and the aurora australis in the south. The colours come from different gases glowing, green being the most common.

## Leap year
A leap year has 366 days, with an extra day added to February. It happens because Earth takes about 365.25 days to orbit the Sun, so the calendar year is slightly short. A year divisible by 4 is usually a leap year, but century years need divisibility by 400.

## Earth's day and year
Earth rotates once in about 24 hours, which makes a day, and it orbits the Sun once in about 365.25 days, which makes a year. The rotation period against the distant stars is about 23 hours 56 minutes. The extra quarter day is why leap years exist.

## Mars day and year
A day on Mars lasts about 24.6 hours, close to an Earth day. A Martian year lasts about 687 Earth days, almost two Earth years. The planet's axial tilt is similar to Earth's, so it also has seasons.

## Heliocentric and geocentric models
The geocentric model placed Earth at the centre of the universe, with everything orbiting it, and was widely believed in ancient times. The heliocentric model places the Sun at the centre, with Earth and the other planets orbiting it. The heliocentric model was supported by Copernicus, Galileo and Kepler.

## Redshift
Redshift is the stretching of light from an object moving away from us, which shifts its colour towards the red end of the spectrum. Astronomers use it to measure how fast galaxies are receding. The farther a galaxy is, the larger its redshift generally is.

## Solar wind
The solar wind is a stream of charged particles flowing out from the Sun in all directions. It carries the Sun's magnetic field with it and shapes the tails of comets. It can disturb satellites and power grids when it is strong.

## Planet
A planet is a large, nearly round body that orbits the Sun and has cleared its orbit of other objects. There are eight planets in the Solar System, divided into four rocky inner planets and four giant outer planets. Small bodies that do not meet the last condition are called dwarf planets.
